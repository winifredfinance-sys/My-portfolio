Product definition

Brainbox Ninja is a free puzzle game for Nigerian players of every age. It is designed to give people a few minutes of focused thinking instead of another scroll.

WhoWhat it is designed to doPalette
Kids 4–10Build memory and focus as they growCandy Brain
Teens and young adultsRebuild attention that heavy social media use wears downNaija Green
AdultsA quick, satisfying mental workoutNaija Green
Adults 60+Keep the mind activeNaija Green

"Designed to," not "proven to." I make no cognitive-benefit claims.

The problem framing

Most brain games pick one audience. A family has several. I wanted one product that a parent, their child and their own parent could each open and immediately find something pitched at their level, with nothing in the way: no download, no account, no ads.

Key product decisions

  • Pick an age band instead of typing an age. One tap is faster than a form, and the game does not ask anyone, especially a child, for personal data.
  • Puzzle categories inside each band, such as maths and proverbs, so players choose what they enjoy.
  • Beginner to hard levels, with up to 10 rounds per level. Finishing one round is not finishing the level, which gives a clear sense of progress.
  • A timer on every round, to create focus and a reason to come back and beat it.
  • A nickname leaderboard, for competition without accounts.
  • Encouraging shout-outs with a suggested next step, such as another puzzle type or another age band, so a finished game leads somewhere.
  • Distinct palettes per audience: Candy Brain for kids, Naija Green for teens, adults and 60+.

Constraints

Free to play. No sign-up. No ads. No downloads. Hosted as a static site on Vercel, so it costs nothing to run and loads fast. These constraints are the product promise, and they decide what does not get built.

How I built it

I defined the product, wrote the requirements and directed AI-assisted development to build it. I judged each version against the requirements and decided what shipped. I did not hand-code it, and it is not an AI product: AI was the build tool; the product decisions were mine.

What feedback changed

I shared the game on LinkedIn to get real players' feedback.

  • Questions were repeating. The question bank was too small for the number of rounds, so I am expanding the content.
  • Players asked for difficulty that adapts to them. Adaptive difficulty is planned as the next major change.

What's next

  • Expand the question bank across every age band and puzzle type.
  • Add adaptive difficulty.
  • Decide on a shared online leaderboard. A hosted database such as Supabase is under consideration; it is not decided, because it adds cost and data to look after.
  • Put a measurement plan in place.

What I learned

Ship, then let users correct you. I would not have guessed that question repetition would be the first complaint. Putting a live product in front of people found it in days.

AI makes building cheap, so judgment is the scarce part. The hard work was deciding who it is for, what to leave out, and what to fix first.
